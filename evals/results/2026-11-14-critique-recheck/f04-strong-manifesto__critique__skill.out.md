## Reader's experience

The second line — "Not because slow is a virtue" — refuses the platitude the whole genre is built on, which buys attention for everything after it. By the end the reader has a picture of a failure (a joint, on a descent, eleven years out), a reason steel is the choice, a sense of what the fitting will be like, and a physical thing they could go and verify on their own bike. Nobody stops reading; a reader in a hurry is told to leave, which is the intended outcome rather than a leak.

## Verdict: Ship

Nothing in the copy needs changing: the differentiating idea is ownable, the proof is physical and checkable, and the voice is unmistakable. Highest-leverage next step is outside the text — confirm the page around it gives a convinced reader somewhere to go.

## What works (keep these)

- **"Not because slow is a virtue. Because a frame brazed in an afternoon is a frame brazed in a hurry, and hurry shows up eleven years later, at a joint, on a descent."** — Converts a value anyone could assert into a failure the reader can picture, with a consequence they can feel. This is the paragraph that earns the rest.
- **"Every frame leaves with the builder's initials under the bottom bracket... That person will know who to blame. We'd like them to have nothing to say."** — The differentiator as a physical fact plus a standard stated by understatement. Visualizable, falsifiable (unscrew the bracket and look), and nobody else is saying it. This is the line a reader repeats to a friend.
- **"If you want it next week, we're the wrong shop. Good. There are other shops."** — Deliberate repulsion, and the one-word "Good." carries the shop's temperament more efficiently than a paragraph of description could. Any revision that softens this loses the piece's nerve.

Also load-bearing: **"We like materials that warn you"** turns a material spec into a philosophy, and **"Twelve frames a month. No more."** ends on a hard fact instead of an aphorism.

## Not flagged

Fragments and one-word sentences ("Good.", "Twelve frames a month. No more.") are deliberate cadence in a manifesto. "Eleven years" is a scene detail, not a statistic — the odd specific number is what makes it read as lived experience rather than a stat, so it stays. "We use steel." is the only sentence a competitor could sign unchanged, and it works as a three-word setup for the two lines that differentiate; no change recommended. Absence of founder names, location, pricing and a CTA fails the additive test: the manifesto's scope is belief.

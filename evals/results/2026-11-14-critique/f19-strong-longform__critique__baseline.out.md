**Verdict:** This is working. The opening earns its place — it has a real organizing image (the ninety seconds of dead air), a character established through specific competence rather than description, and a quote that states the feature's subject without announcing it. I'd push back on very little. The notes below are ranked, and most are small.

**What's carrying the piece**

The ninety-second gap is the right choice of entry point: it's a hole in the thing the reader assumes is seamless, and it's small enough to be literal. "A handset older than his apprentice" does three jobs in six words — equipment, tenure, succession. And "arrive lit like apartment blocks and leave dark, because by the time they leave, everyone aboard who can sleep is asleep" is the best sentence here; the image and the explanation are the same move, and "who can sleep" opens a door without walking through it.

**Notes, in order of consequence**

1. **Check whether the central claim is literally true.** The opening says "nothing moves at all" and the close says "the port started again" — the whole port, waiting on one man's handset. If that's accurate, it's a superb detail and the piece is built correctly. If it's a compression of something looser (his crane, his row, his section), then the most load-bearing image in the opening is the one that overclaims, and a reader who knows ports will catch it. Either confirm it or narrow the frame. This is the only note I'd call important.

2. **Okonjo's job arrives late.** Paragraph 1 has him on a radio; paragraph 2 has him knowing cranes by sound; only "the first night I rode up with him" tells us he's in a cab. Readers spend two paragraphs with a slightly blurry man. A couple of words in paragraph 1 — the cab, the ladder, the height — would fix it without spending the reveal.

3. **"Leave" twice in one sentence.** "…and leave dark, because by the time they leave…" The sentence is otherwise the strongest in the piece; "because by then everyone aboard who can sleep is asleep" keeps the rhythm and loses the echo.

4. **"It's a different job" is the quote explaining itself.** "Nights, you're moving boxes and nobody's watching" has already landed it. I'd still probably keep the line — it's how people actually talk, and three beats close better than two — but know that you're paying a small tax in on-the-noseness for the cadence.

5. **"Which one only pretends to"** is the one place the writer's voice is more audible than Okonjo's. It's good, and it's doing real work on the intimacy of his knowledge. Keep it, but it's the flourish to sacrifice first if a later paragraph adds a second one.

6. **Low priority: no scale yet.** We have sound and light and nothing else — no cold, no size, no sense of how many people are out there with him. That's defensible restraint for an opening, but if the next section doesn't supply it quickly, the port stays an idea rather than a place.

**What I'd leave alone**

"About ninety seconds" — resist the urge to make it exact; the hedge reads as honest rather than vague. The structure, including the return to the frequency at the end; the circle closes without calling attention to itself. And the apprentice, mentioned once and dropped — assuming he comes back later, that's a seed, not a loose end.

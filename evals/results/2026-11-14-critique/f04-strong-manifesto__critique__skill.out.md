## Intent read

**Piece:** Six-paragraph brand manifesto, About page, custom bicycle frame shop · **Profile:** 2 Brand manifesto (S2/L3/L4 Critical; S1/T1/L2 Important) · **Reader:** a rider considering a custom steel frame, mostly solution-aware — knows custom builders exist, is choosing between them · **Job:** make the right rider trust the shop and the wrong rider leave without resentment; justify the wait and the price without arguing for them · **Constraints:** About page, no CTA expected, no BRAND.md supplied (voice inferred from the piece) · **Confidence:** high on profile, medium on reader mix.

## Reader's experience

The shop has a position and will say no to you, and both of those make the work more believable rather than less. The reader takes away one image — initials under the bottom bracket, for the mechanic, not for them — and one argument: hurry is invisible now and visible in eleven years, on a descent. The two places a reader might go flat are the steel paragraph, which says what every steel builder says, and the closing number, which they can't tell is small.

## Verdict: Revise

Revise from a high base: the thesis, voice and distinctiveness are all working, but one paragraph reverts to category language on a Critical dimension, and the closing proof point arrives without the baseline that makes it land. Both fixes are content, not phrasing — nothing else should move.

**Highest-leverage next step:** rewrite ¶3 (steel) so it carries something only this shop could say. It sits where the buyer's second question lands, and it's the only paragraph a competitor could sign unchanged.

## What works (keep these)

- **"Not because slow is a virtue. Because a frame brazed in an afternoon is a frame brazed in a hurry, and hurry shows up eleven years later, at a joint, on a descent."** (¶2) — The piece uses a cliché, refuses it in the next breath, and replaces it with a mechanism and a consequence the reader feels under their own body. This is the engine; everything after it is credible because of it.
- **"Every frame leaves with the builder's initials under the bottom bracket, where nobody will see them but the next person who fixes it. That person will know who to blame. We'd like them to have nothing to say."** (¶5) — Accountability shown through a practice instead of asserted through adjectives, and aimed at an audience the customer will never be. The understatement in the last sentence does what a superlative would have destroyed.
- **"If you want it next week, we're the wrong shop. Good. There are other shops."** (¶4) — Self-selection that visibly costs the shop money, which is what buys the rest of the page its authority.
- **"We like materials that warn you."** (¶3) — The ownable half of the weakest paragraph. Any fix to ¶3 must keep this line.

## Findings

```
[P1 · Strategy · Positioning & differentiation] ¶3 is the one paragraph a competitor could sign unchanged
  Evidence:       "We use steel. Steel bends before it breaks, and it tells you first." (line 5, ¶3)
  Why it matters: A solution-aware reader is comparing steel builders, not deciding between
                  steel and carbon. Steel's failure mode is shared trade knowledge — every
                  builder in the category says it, so the sentence tells this reader nothing
                  about this shop. It also leaves an implied comparison unanchored (bends
                  before what breaks?), which is the only soft claim on the page. The cost is
                  position: the paragraph carrying the material argument is the one that
                  sounds generic.
  Intervention:   Replace the shared fact with something from this shop's own experience of
                  steel, and keep "We like materials that warn you" as the landing. Content
                  fix, not a rephrase.
  Example (A, needs nothing from the author — cut the generic sentence):
                  "We use steel. We like materials that warn you."
  Example (B, stronger, with the shop's own evidence):
                  "We use steel. [NEEDS: one thing this shop has actually seen steel do — a
                  cracked frame that still got its rider home, a tube that dented instead of
                  folding, a repair made twenty years after the braze.] We like materials
                  that warn you."
```

```
[P1 · Structure · Argument & hierarchy] The closing number has no baseline, so the refusal may not read as one
  Evidence:       "Twelve frames a month. No more." (line 11, ¶6)
  Why it matters: The line's whole force depends on the reader reading twelve as a ceiling
                  the shop pays for. A rider who doesn't know frame-shop economics has no
                  way to judge: twelve a month is roughly a frame every second working day,
                  which can read as a lot of output from a page that opened on slowness.
                  This is the one place the piece doesn't do what ¶2 does so well — ¶2
                  refuses to let "slowly" stand as a bare word and supplies the mechanism;
                  ¶6 lets "twelve" stand as a bare number in the emphasis position.
  Intervention:   Give the number its denominator. One clause, same rhythm.
  Example:        "Twelve frames a month, between [NEEDS: number of builders]. No more."
                  Optional second specific, if the shop will commit to it in writing:
                  the current wait — "[NEEDS: current wait, in months]" — which this reader
                  is already asking after ¶4 told them not to expect next week.
```

## Preferences and alternatives

- **[P3]** "eleven years later" reads as earned experience precisely because it isn't "ten." If there is a real frame behind it, naming the failure — not the year — would be stronger still: `[NEEDS: the repair or failure this line is drawn from]`. Defensible exactly as written; a manifesto's precision is rhetorical, not statistical.

## Not flagged

Fragments and one-sentence paragraphs ("Twelve frames a month. No more.") are deliberate cadence in a manifesto, as is the absence of benefits, pricing or a call to action. The two refusals (¶4 and ¶6) are escalation, not redundancy — ¶4 turns away the impatient buyer, ¶6 states the structural limit; cutting either weakens the other. "Good." stays: the bluntness is the brand, not a lapse in it. "Nothing to say" carries a sliver of ambiguity that the understatement earns.

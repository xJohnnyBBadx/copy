## Intent read

**Piece:** Homepage hero, a three-item "Why Nimbus?" feature block, and a closing CTA (129 words).
**Profile:** 3 · Website & landing page. Critical tiers: S1, S2, S3, T1, T2, L1. Important: L2, L3. Secondary: L4.
**Reader:** Team leads actively comparing project-management tools — **solution-aware**. They know the category, have two or three competitors open in other tabs, and are looking for the reason to pick one.
**Job:** For the reader, decide whether Nimbus is worth a trial over the tools they're already evaluating. For the author, earn that trial.
**Constraints:** Short-form hero + features; no BRAND.md found (voice inferred from the piece); no proof assets, customer names, or product details supplied.
**Confidence:** High on profile and reader. Low on what Nimbus actually does differently — the page does not say, and I will not guess.

## Reader's experience

A team lead arrives mid-comparison and meets a headline about unlocking potential, then a sentence telling them the world is fast-paced. By the end of 129 words they have not learned what Nimbus is — the word "project" never appears — nor one thing it does that the tools in their other tabs don't. The two moments that try to build trust ("Trusted by leading teams worldwide" and "Studies show…") are the moments a buyer at this stage discounts hardest, so the page's credibility is lowest exactly where it reaches for proof. Most such readers leave at the hero.

## Verdict: **Rethink**

The page fails the swap test outright, never names its own category, and both of its proof points are borrowed authority. Sentence work is premature; there is no promise on the page to sharpen.

**Highest-leverage next step:** Write one sentence a competitor could not sign — what Nimbus does that Asana, Linear, or Monday cannot, stated as a mechanism rather than a benefit — then rebuild the hero and all three bullets from that sentence. Everything else here is downstream of it. If that sentence can't be written yet, the problem is positioning, not copy, and no rewrite will cover it.

## What works (keep these)

- **"eliminate busywork"** — the only phrase on the page that names a consequence a team lead actually feels. It is the seed of a real benefit line. Keep the thought; drop the "Leverage cutting-edge AI to" that precedes it.
- **The "Why Nimbus?" three-bullet scaffold** — the right architecture for this reader at this point on the page. A comparison shopper wants exactly three scannable reasons. The frame is sound; only its contents fail. A revision should refill these bullets, not replace the structure.
- **Short, plain sentences** (mean 9.3 words, no stacked clauses, no jargon walls). The reading level is correct for a skimmed hero. Nothing needs mechanical smoothing, and a reviser should resist adding sophistication here.

## Findings

**[P0 · Strategy · Positioning & differentiation] No differentiating idea: every line survives a name swap.**
  Evidence: "Nimbus is the all-in-one, AI-powered platform that empowers teams to collaborate seamlessly, streamline workflows, and achieve more." (line 3); "Seamless Collaboration… Powerful Automation… Actionable Insights" (lines 7–9)
  Why it matters: I tried to state the central promise in one sentence and could only produce a category description. Substitute Asana, ClickUp, Monday, or Notion for Nimbus and not one word breaks — including the bullets, which name the three things every tool in this category claims. For a solution-aware reader whose entire task is telling these products apart, a page that describes the category hands the decision to whoever else gives them a reason. "AI-powered" and "cutting-edge AI" no longer carry weight either; in 2026 they mark the category rather than distinguish within it.
  Intervention: Strategic, not editorial. Find the mechanism — the specific thing in how Nimbus works that competitors cannot copy by next quarter — and make it the hero. One sharp claim beats three generic pillars.
  Example: The shape, since the content isn't in the source — "Nimbus is project management that [mechanism]. [What the team lead can stop doing as a result.]" `[NEEDS: the one capability Nimbus has that Asana, Linear and Monday do not — stated as how it works, not as a benefit]`

**[P0 · Strategy · Purpose & reader fit] The page never says what Nimbus is, and addresses an unaware reader rather than a comparing one.**
  Evidence: "In today's fast-paced world, teams need more than just a tool. They need a partner." (line 3); "Whether you're a startup or an enterprise" (line 13). "Project," "task," "deadline," "sprint," and "roadmap" appear nowhere.
  Why it matters: Two separate failures with one effect. First, the page is category-illegible: "collaborate," "workflows," and "insights" span chat tools, automation platforms, and BI dashboards, and the piece calls Nimbus five different things in 129 words — "a tool," "a partner," "platform," "software," "a new way of working." A reader trying to work out which shortlist Nimbus belongs on cannot. Second, it opens by explaining that teams need tools, which is education for someone who doesn't yet know the category exists. This reader is three tabs deep. "Whether you're a startup or an enterprise" then widens the target to everyone, which tells a team lead the product was built for nobody in particular.
  Intervention: Name the category in the hero, pick one term for the product and keep it, and cut the world-is-fast-paced opening — a solution-aware reader needs difference, not context.
  Example: "Nimbus is project management for `[NEEDS: the specific team this is built for — e.g. the 8-person product team, the 200-person agency]`." Then delete lines 3's first two sentences entirely.

**[P0 · Strategy · Credibility & claim integrity] Both proof points on the page are borrowed authority, and one argues for the competition.**
  Evidence: "Trusted by leading teams worldwide" (line 11); "Studies show that teams using collaboration tools are more productive than ever." (line 13)
  Why it matters: "Leading… worldwide" is an unsupported superlative doing the credibility work for the whole page — no names, no number, no logos — and it is the single most discounted phrase in B2B software copy. The research sentence is worse: unnamed source, and "more productive than ever" has no comparison class (than whom? than when?). Read at its most generous it is a claim about *collaboration tools as a category*, so it makes the case for every competitor in the reader's other tabs as readily as for Nimbus, while importing a substantiation risk. A hollow proof line costs more than a missing one, because it tells the reader proof was reached for and not found. Claim inventory: "the all-in-one, AI-powered platform," "collaborate seamlessly," and "cutting-edge AI" all sit at rung 3 of the evidence ladder — plausible, unsupported, asserted with adjectives.
  Intervention: Delete the research sentence. Replace the trust line with specifics the author already holds, or cut it until they do. Do not restate either claim more carefully; the fix is evidence or silence.
  Example: Cut line 13's second sentence. For line 11: `[NEEDS: 3–5 named customers cleared for use, or the number of paying teams]`. If category data is genuinely wanted: `[NEEDS: named study, publisher, year, and the specific metric measured]`.

**[P1 · Structure · Argument & hierarchy] "Why Nimbus?" is followed by three answers that aren't about Nimbus.**
  Evidence: "## Why Nimbus?" → "Bring your whole team together in one place." / "Leverage cutting-edge AI to eliminate busywork." / "Harness the power of data to make smarter decisions." (lines 5–9)
  Why it matters: The heading makes the page's only explicit promise of differentiation, and the content beneath it lists three table-stakes capabilities. A reader who scans the heading, scans the bullets, and finds nothing Nimbus-specific concludes the answer to "why Nimbus" is "no particular reason." The heading raises the bar the bullets then fail to clear, so this structure is actively more damaging than an unlabelled feature list would be.
  Intervention: Keep the heading and the three slots. Each bullet should carry one concrete, checkable thing — a named workflow, an integration, a number, a before-and-after — and at least one should be something only Nimbus can claim.

**[P1 · Structure · Persuasive design] No objection this reader actually has is addressed, and the ask hides its own terms.**
  Evidence: "Ready to transform the way you work? Get started today." (line 15)
  Why it matters: A team lead comparing tools brings a predictable sequence of questions: what is it, how is it different, show me it working, will my team adopt it, what does migrating off our current tool cost, what's the price, what happens if I try it. The page answers none of the middle five and asks on the last. "Get started today" names no friction — free trial or demo? card required? how long until my team is in it? — so the reader must assume the most expensive possible answer. In a category where switching cost is the real objection, leaving migration and adoption unmentioned reads as not having an answer.
  Intervention: Add the two objections that actually block this reader (switching/migration, and team adoption), and state the trial terms inside the CTA so the ask carries its own risk reversal.
  Example: "Start free — `[NEEDS: trial length, and whether a card is required]`." Then, next to it, the migration answer: `[NEEDS: what import from the competitor's tool actually involves, and how long it takes]`.

**[P1 · Structure · Argument & hierarchy] A quarter of the page restates the headline instead of advancing it.**
  Evidence: "Unlock Your Team's Full Potential" (line 1); "and achieve more" (line 3); "It's a new way of working." (line 11); "elevate your productivity and unlock new possibilities" (line 13); "Ready to transform the way you work?" and "The future of work is here." (line 15)
  Why it matters: Six phrasings of one empty promise, roughly 34 of 129 words. On a page this short that is the space the differentiator, the proof, and the migration answer all needed. The closing line also lands on a generic ending that could close any technology page, where the last thing the reader reads should be the most specific thing on the page.
  Intervention: Keep one statement of the promise — in the hero — and spend the reclaimed words on specifics. End on the concrete next step and its terms, not on the future of work.

**[P2 · Language · Specificity] Eleven stock-vocabulary hits in 129 words, and no fact anywhere for them to sit on.**
  Evidence: "unlock" (×2), "fast-paced world," "empowers," "seamlessly," "streamline," "Seamless," "Leverage," "cutting-edge," "Harness the power," "elevate" — plus two reveal constructions ("teams need more than just a tool. They need a partner," line 3; "isn't just software. It's a new way of working," line 11) and a hedge that softens an already-empty promise ("can help you elevate your productivity," line 13).
  Why it matters: Not a style complaint. This vocabulary is the mechanism by which the page avoids saying anything falsifiable: there is not one number, customer name, integration, screenshot reference, or named task in the piece. A specific claim gets believed or checked; a general one gets skipped. Both reveal constructions also inflate — they set up a straw man ("just a tool," "just software") to promote the product above its own category, which a buyer reads as puffery.
  Intervention: Replace each adjective with the fact that would have justified it. Swapping "leverage" for "use" or "seamless" for "smooth" changes nothing; the cause is the missing fact, not the word.
  Example: "Leverage cutting-edge AI to eliminate busywork" → name the busywork. `[NEEDS: the one task the AI actually removes — status updates, triage, scheduling, something a team lead recognizes from last Tuesday]`

I also left the three-bullet form alone: three feature areas is a genuine list, not a reflexive triplet, and the problem is the bullets' content. Short sentences and the plain register are right for a hero and should not be evened out or elevated.

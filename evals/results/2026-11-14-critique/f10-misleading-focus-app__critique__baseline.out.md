# Critique: FocusForge landing page

## Bottom line

This page cannot ship. It is eleven lines long and contains at least nine claims that the company's own fact sheet contradicts or fails to support. The problem is not tone or polish — it's that the copy asserts things the company knows to be untrue, including two that are directly refuted by its own analytics and one that invents a clinical trial out of nothing.

Separately, and almost as damaging: **the page never says what the product is.** Strip the claims and there's no indication FocusForge is a focus timer. Every line here would work verbatim for a nootropic supplement, a meditation course, or an MLM. That's a positioning failure independent of the accuracy failure.

---

## Claim-by-claim against the facts

| Line | Claim | What the facts say |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Double your focus in 7 days" | 62% of 140 surveyed users *reported* better focus. "Better" ≠ doubled, and focus has no unit that can be doubled. |
| 1 | "Guaranteed" | No guarantee exists in the fact sheet. This is a warranty promise with no stated terms, refund path, or basis. |
| 3 | "clinically proven" | **There is no clinical trial.** An internal customer survey is not a clinical study. |
| 3 | "the only app" | No competitive claim is supported. Sole-provider claims invite a direct challenge from any competitor. |
| 3 | "up to 247%" | This number appears nowhere in the source facts. It has no origin. |
| 5 | "Join 2 million high performers who have already transformed their productivity" | 2 million is **downloads**, not members. ~60,000 are monthly actives — roughly 3%. The page describes 97% of that number as having "transformed their productivity" when they don't open the app. |
| 5 | "neuroscience-backed method rewires your brain" | No mechanism evidence provided. "Rewires your brain" is a neurological claim. |
| 5 | "in just 10 minutes a day" | Not in the fact set. May be true — it isn't sourced. |
| 7 | "I finished my novel in three weeks" | Not in the fact set, and an extreme outlier result presented as representative, with no typicality disclaimer. |
| 9 | "Only 37 founding-member spots left... Offer ends at midnight" | **The founding-member price has no end date.** Both the count and the deadline are fabricated. |

Line 9 is the most legally exposed sentence on the page, because its falsity is provable by a single screenshot taken tomorrow. A fake countdown on a price that never expires is a per-se deceptive practice in most jurisdictions that regulate advertising at all, and the two scarcity devices are *stacked* — a limited quantity and a limited time — which doubles the exposure for no added persuasion.

"Clinically proven" is the second: it's a term of art, it's false, and it is the kind of claim that gets an app pulled from the App Store independent of any regulator.

## Craft problems that survive even a fully factual rewrite

**The persuasion tactics are miscalibrated to the stated audience.** The page addresses "high performers" — people who evaluate tools skeptically and have seen a thousand midnight deadlines — and then deploys the least sophisticated conversion devices available: a fake timer, a round-number vanity metric, a percentage with a suspiciously precise decimal-free 247, and an anonymized miracle testimonial. For this reader, each device is a tell. The copy is simultaneously illegal and ineffective, which is an unusual achievement.

**"Up to 247%" and "Guaranteed" contradict each other.** A guarantee is a floor; "up to" is a ceiling with no floor. Putting them two lines apart tells the reader the writer doesn't believe either one.

**The 247% figure is self-defeating even if it were real.** Precision implies measurement; a reader who believes the number will ask how focus was quantified. There is no answer on the page.

**"At this price" — but no price is given.** The offer cannot be evaluated. The CTA ("Claim my spot") is load-bearing on the fake scarcity: remove line 9 and the button loses its referent.

**Zero product specificity.** No screenshot described, no feature named, no session length, no platform, no description of what happens when you open it. A focus timer has concrete, demonstrable mechanics — intervals, blocking, streaks, reports. None appear. The page sells a brain transformation and omits the thing being sold.

**Stock-phrase density.** "High performers," "transformed their productivity," "neuroscience-backed," "deep work," "rewires your brain" — five borrowed phrases in one 34-word paragraph. None carries information.

## What you actually have to work with

The honest facts are weaker than the copy pretends but stronger than they look:

- **60,000 monthly active users** is a better number than 2 million downloads. It's specific, unusual (most pages round or inflate), it survives scrutiny, and it signals retention rather than install churn — which is precisely what a skeptical buyer of a focus app wants to know.
- **62% of 140 users reported better focus after a week** can be used, but only with the methodology attached in the sentence itself: internal survey, sample size, self-reported. Note the real limits before someone else does: n=140, no control group, no baseline measure, and survey respondents self-select toward engaged users. It will not carry a headline. It can support a modest, attributed line.
- **A founding-member price with no end date** is a genuine, permanent offer. That's a real benefit — say so plainly. "Founding-member pricing, locked for as long as you stay subscribed" is more compelling to this audience than a countdown, because it's a commitment rather than a threat.

A defensible headline makes a claim about the product's behavior, not the user's neurology — something like what the timer actually does differently from the fifty free ones. That's the brief the author hasn't answered yet, and no amount of claim-scrubbing substitutes for it.

---

## Recommended disposition

Don't edit this draft. The structure is built on the fabricated claims — pull them and nothing load-bearing remains. Rewrite from the product, with these hard constraints:

1. Delete "clinically proven," "the only app," "247%," "Guaranteed," and "rewires your brain" outright. No softened variants ("clinically studied," "scientifically designed," "up to 2x") — hedged versions of unsupported claims carry the same exposure with less conviction.
2. Replace the 2 million figure with 60,000 monthly actives, or state "2 million downloads" and nothing more.
3. Cut lines 7 and 9 entirely. If a testimonial is used, it must be a real, attributable, typical-case quote.
4. Add, in the first 20 words, what FocusForge is and what it does.
5. Any statistic that stays must carry its source in the same sentence a reader's eye crosses.

One process note worth raising with the company: the 247% figure and Dana R.'s novel did not come from the fact sheet, which means they came from somewhere else. Before the rewrite, it's worth establishing where — if either traces to a real source, it changes what's usable; if neither does, the review process that let them into a draft needs attention more than the copy does.

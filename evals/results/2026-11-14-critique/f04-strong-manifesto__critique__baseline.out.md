## Verdict

This is strong, and the main risk to it is over-editing. The voice is consistent, the time horizon (eleven years out, the next mechanic) organizes the whole piece, and the central move — refusing the artisanal cliché in line 3 before anyone can accuse you of it — is what buys the authority everything else spends. Three things are worth touching. Most of it should be left alone.

## What's load-bearing (don't let an edit pass flatten these)

- **"Not because slow is a virtue."** This is the hinge of the entire manifesto. It preempts the one objection the reader is already forming, and it converts slowness from a pose into a consequence. Without it, line 1 is every frame builder's About page.
- **"eleven years later, at a joint, on a descent."** The three-beat narrowing, and the fact that the crash is never named. "Eleven" rather than "ten" is doing real work — unround numbers read as recalled, round ones as invented.
- **"We like materials that warn you."** Six words that generalize a material choice into a worldview, and indict carbon fiber without naming it. Naming the competitor would be weaker.
- **"argued with you once."** The surprising verb in line 7 and the piece's best characterization. It qualifies the audience more efficiently than any paragraph about "our ideal client" could.
- **The turn in line 9, from "blame" to "nothing to say."** The emotional center. Note that "We'd like" is softer than every other verb in the piece — that's correct. Humility lands at the one place it belongs. Don't harden it to match the surrounding declaratives.

## Three things to fix

**1. "Good." — cut it.** This is the only false note in the piece. Everywhere else, you earn authority by stating a fact and letting the consequence sit there unstated. Here you editorialize about the reader walking out, and the voice tips from confident into pleased with itself. "There are other shops" already does the dismissal, and does it gracefully. The context sharpens the cost: an About page is read by someone deciding whether to commit four figures and a months-long wait, often on a first visit. A flash of smugness at exactly that moment is the most expensive sentence on the page. Cutting one word leaves: *"If you want it next week, we're the wrong shop. There are other shops."* Same message, no smirk. (It also relieves the doubled "shop," which currently reads as performance rather than echo.)

**2. "Twelve frames a month" reads two ways, and you only want one of them.** To a reader who has just absorbed five paragraphs about slowness and one builder's initials, 144 frames a year may land as a production shop rather than a restraint — which undercuts the claim instead of capping it. The number is also a hard, checkable, dateable commitment sitting in evergreen copy: if you ever build fourteen, this becomes the one provable falsehood on the site, on the page whose entire job is credibility. Consider whether the honest unit is per-builder, or a figure with more obvious scarcity in it. If twelve is genuinely the cap and you intend to hold it for years, keep it — just make the choice deliberately rather than inheriting it.

**3. Line 1 says "bicycles." You build frames.** The rest of the piece is scrupulous about this — "frame" in lines 3, 9, and 11 — which makes the opening the single place the language goes loose, at the most-read sentence on the page. If you sell frames, "We build bicycle frames slowly" costs one word and removes a small, early reason to doubt the precision you're about to claim. If you do build complete bikes, ignore this.

## Two to check against your actual process

**"A frame brazed in an afternoon is a frame brazed in a hurry."** This is the piece's one unverifiable technical assertion, and it's load-bearing — the whole argument for slowness rests on it. A reader who actually knows framebuilding may object that a skilled builder can braze a main triangle in an afternoon without hurrying, and if they do, the sentence they catch you on is the sentence holding up the manifesto. I can't adjudicate this from the copy alone; you can. If the real constraint is somewhere else — alignment, fitting, cure and check cycles, the time between operations — naming that constraint instead would be both truer and more interesting, because it's specific to your bench rather than generic to the genre.

**"The builder's initials"** implies a shop with more than one builder, which squares nicely with twelve a month. Just confirm that's the picture you want, since much of the surrounding voice ("we've measured you twice") invites the reader to imagine one person.

## One page-level question

As written, this is a manifesto, not an About page. A reader finishing line 11 does not know who you are, where you are, how long you've been doing this, or what to do next — and line 9 is precisely the moment someone decides they want one. If this is the top of the page and names, location, history, and a way to start a conversation follow below, it's doing its job and the restraint is right. If this *is* the whole page, the gap is real: the manifesto has converted the reader and then given them nowhere to go. Don't fix it by appending a paragraph in this register — anything that tries to match this voice while delivering logistics will read as a weaker imitation of it. Let the facts be plain and unglamorous underneath, and let the contrast work for you.

The night shift at the Port of Coldharbour begins at eleven, when the gantry cranes switch from the day crew's radio channel to the night crew's, and for about ninety seconds — the time it takes Ray Okonjo to find the right frequency on a handset older than his apprentice — nothing moves at all.

Okonjo has worked nights here for nineteen years. He knows which crane groans in a north wind and which one only pretends to. He knows that the container ships arrive lit like apartment blocks and leave dark, because by the time they leave, everyone aboard who can sleep is asleep.

"Days, you're moving boxes," he told me, the first night I rode up with him. "Nights, you're moving boxes and nobody's watching. It's a different job." Then he found the frequency, and the port started again.

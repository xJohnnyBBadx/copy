# Writing-context profiles

Pick one profile during the intent read. If a piece mixes contexts (a launch post that is half thought leadership), pick the one that matches the reader's main job and note the other. Read the matrix row and that profile's section only.

**Tiers:** **C** Critical · **I** Important · **S** Secondary · **–** n/a. Rubric anchors and verdict rules: [rubric.md](rubric.md).

## 1 · Positioning & messaging
Positioning statements, messaging frameworks, value propositions, boilerplate, internal briefs.
- **Good looks like:** one claim a competitor could not sign; a named reader; proof points that exist; language precise enough that a copywriter could brief from it.
- **Lenses:** swap test, Ogilvy positioning → promise, Schwartz sophistication ([lenses.md](lenses.md)).
- **Don't flag:** plain, unadorned language (an internal document is a tool, not an ad); lack of a call to action; absence of emotion.
- **Watch for:** three or more "pillars" that are each category-generic; differentiators that are really features of every competitor ("easy to use", "secure"); positioning by aspiration with no proof.

## 2 · Brand manifesto
Belief statements, founding letters, "what we stand for."
- **Good looks like:** a belief worth disagreeing with; a recognizable voice; concrete moments that earn the abstraction; a build toward something.
- **Lenses:** narrative arc, swap test (a manifesto any company could publish has failed).
- **Don't flag:** fragments, anaphora, repetition, rhythm devices, deliberate abstraction, length, the absence of a CTA or benefits, unconventional punctuation that is consistent.
- **Watch for:** beliefs nobody would oppose ("we believe in quality"); factual or performance claims smuggled in as beliefs (treat those under [claims.md](claims.md)); a manifesto that is really a feature list.

## 3 · Website & landing page
Homepages, landing pages, pricing pages, product pages.
- **Good looks like:** the reader knows within seconds what this is, who it's for, and why it's different; proof sits next to claims; objections are answered in the order they arise; one clear next step that fits the reader's readiness.
- **Lenses:** Schwartz awareness (what must the hero do for a reader at this level?), Dry's three questions, Ogilvy facts over adjectives, so-what chain.
- **Don't flag:** a long page for a considered purchase (length is fine when every section answers a real question); a short page for a simple offer.
- **Watch for:** hero lines that describe the category rather than the difference; feature lists with no consequence for the reader; "trusted by" logos with no specifics; several competing calls to action.

## 4 · Advertising campaign
Print, out-of-home, digital and social ads, campaign platforms, radio.
- **Good looks like:** one idea, executed so that it is noticed and remembered; the brand is unmistakable; the promise is relevant to the reader.
- **Lenses:** Ogilvy big idea and headline rules, Hopkins specificity, swap test.
- **Don't flag:** wordplay, ambiguity, or a missing explicit benefit when the idea carries the benefit; unconventional form; long copy in print when the reader is interested.
- **Watch for:** cleverness that hides the brand or the promise; comparative or superlative claims (regulated in most markets; flag for substantiation); executions that would work equally well for a competitor.

## 5a · Headline
Headlines, subject lines, hero lines, H1s.
- **Good looks like:** the right reader stops and reads on; the promise or news is legible in about two seconds; concrete beats clever unless the clever version is also clear.
- **Lenses:** Ogilvy headline rules, Dry's three questions, Schwartz awareness (an unaware reader needs a different headline from a most-aware one).
- **Don't flag:** a long headline that carries a specific fact; a question headline that the target reader would answer "yes" to.
- **Watch for:** two ideas joined with "and"; a headline that only makes sense after the body copy; curiosity with no payoff.

## 5b · Tagline
Brand lines meant to be repeated for years.
- **Good looks like:** distils the positioning; sounds like the brand; short enough to repeat; holds up after the hundredth time.
- **Don't flag:** abstraction, compression, or the absence of a literal benefit; a tagline isn't a headline.
- **Watch for:** lines that could belong to any brand ("Innovation for life"); lines that need explaining; puns that date quickly.

## 6 · Corporate communications
Announcements, press releases, internal memos, all-hands notes, policy changes, crisis and bad-news communications.
- **Good looks like:** the news in the first two sentences; what changes for the reader and when; honest about bad news; a named owner or next step.
- **Lenses:** inverted pyramid, BLUF, plain language.
- **Don't flag:** required legal or forward-looking statements; plainness; the lack of brand flourish.
- **Watch for:** euphemism that hides the news ("rightsizing", "evolving our structure"): if it misleads the people affected, that is P0. Passive voice that hides who decided. Quotes from executives that say nothing.

## 7 · Investor communications
Pitch decks, investor letters and updates, shareholder letters, memos.
- **Good looks like:** every number sourced or clearly labelled as an estimate; the thesis in one sentence; risks acknowledged; the narrative supported by data rather than adjectives.
- **Lenses:** SCQA (situation, complication, question, answer), claims evidence ladder, Ogilvy facts.
- **Don't flag:** dryness; dense data where the reader expects it.
- **Watch for (often P0):** market-size claims with no method; implied guarantees of returns; superlatives ("the only", "the leading") without basis; forward-looking statements presented as fact; cherry-picked time windows. Recommend legal review for securities-related language; do not give legal advice.

## 8 · Product marketing
Feature pages, launch posts, release notes for customers, one-pagers, sales enablement, comparison pages.
- **Good looks like:** each feature tied to a consequence the reader cares about; honest comparisons; specific proof; clear on what's new.
- **Lenses:** so-what chain, Schwartz awareness, Dry's three questions.
- **Don't flag:** technical terms the target buyer uses.
- **Watch for:** "revolutionary" launches of incremental features; comparison claims without a basis; features listed in engineering order rather than reader order.

## 9 · Social media
Posts, threads, captions, short-form text.
- **Good looks like:** the first line earns the second; sounds like a person or a brand with a point of view; one idea per post; native to the platform.
- **Don't flag:** casual register, fragments, line breaks for rhythm, emoji used consistently with the brand.
- **Watch for:** engagement-bait formulas ("Here's what nobody tells you"), listicle padding, reveal constructions, generic inspiration with no insight.

## 10 · Video & audio scripts
Ads, explainers, narration, podcasts, keynotes. Written for the ear.
- **Good looks like:** short spoken sentences; one idea per beat; signposting where the listener needs it; the key line repeated for retention; reads naturally aloud.
- **Lenses:** read-aloud test, narrative arc.
- **Don't flag:** repetition, signposting, fragments, direct address, contractions, a sentence that begins with "And" or "So".
- **Watch for:** parentheticals and stacked clauses (impossible to hear); numbers and names that can't be followed by ear; visual references that the script doesn't support.

## 11 · Thought leadership
Op-eds, essays, POV papers, executive posts and articles.
- **Good looks like:** a thesis a reasonable person could disagree with; evidence or lived experience behind it; the author's own voice; the reader learns something.
- **Lenses:** obvious-thesis test (if nobody would argue the opposite, it is not a point of view), claims evidence ladder.
- **Don't flag:** first person, strong opinions, informal register.
- **Watch for (P0 for this profile):** a thesis everyone already agrees with; straw-man opponents; trend summaries with no position; borrowed authority ("experts agree").

## 12 · Technical & explanatory
Documentation, how-tos, explainers, white papers, specifications.
- **Good looks like:** task-first organization; consistent terminology; second person and present tense for instructions; defined terms; examples where abstraction would stall the reader.
- **Lenses:** plain-language (Google developer documentation style guide, Microsoft Writing Style Guide), structure by reader task.
- **Don't flag:** necessary jargon for the stated audience; passive voice when the actor is irrelevant ("the file is deleted"); exact repetition of a term (consistency beats variety); lack of persuasion or brand voice. White papers move T2 and S2 to Secondary.
- **Watch for:** synonym cycling for the same concept; steps out of order; marketing language in documentation; undefined acronyms.

## 13 · Long-form editorial
Features, essays, newsletters, books, reported pieces.
- **Good looks like:** an angle; reporting or specifics on every page; a voice; structure that sustains attention; an ending that pays off.
- **Don't flag:** long sentences that read well, em dashes, digressions that pay off, literary devices, the absence of a call to action.
- **Watch for:** facts without sources; scene-setting that delays the point with no reward; a conclusion that restates the introduction.

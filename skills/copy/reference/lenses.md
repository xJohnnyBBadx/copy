# Diagnostic lenses

Compact tools for seeing a problem. Load the lenses the context profile names; don't apply every lens to every piece. They are written in our own words with attribution; the source books are worth reading in full.

## Reader and market

**Awareness levels** (after Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966). How much does the reader already know?
- *Unaware:* doesn't know they have the problem → open with the reader's world or a story, not the product.
- *Problem-aware:* feels the problem, doesn't know solutions exist → open with the problem, named the way they would name it.
- *Solution-aware:* knows solutions exist, not yours → open with what makes yours different.
- *Product-aware:* knows yours, isn't convinced → proof, objections, risk reversal.
- *Most aware:* ready → the offer and the next step, fast.
Diagnosis: most weak openings are written for the wrong level, usually product-aware copy aimed at a problem-aware reader.

**Market sophistication** (also Schwartz). How many similar claims has this reader already heard? In a crowded category, a direct claim ("faster backups") no longer lands. The copy needs a new mechanism (how it works differently) or an identification with the reader instead. Diagnosis: a claim that is true but sounds like everyone else.

## Promise and proof

**Positioning before copy** (after David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising*, 1983). Two decisions come before any copy: what the product does and who it is for, and the single promise. Most weak advertising promises nothing or promises everything. Facts persuade more than adjectives; the reader is intelligent; long copy works for an interested reader. Caveat: Ogilvy's evidence comes mostly from print direct response; brand campaigns and short formats work differently.

**Specificity is believed** (Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising*, 1923, public domain). "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. They leave no impression whatever." A specific claim is taken as true or false; a general one is discounted. Hopkins also argued for service over salesmanship (offer the reader something useful) and for testing rather than opinion.

**The three questions** (after Harry Dry, *Marketing Examples*). For any key line: Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Could nobody else say it? A line that fails all three is abstract, unprovable, and interchangeable. A line that passes all three is usually strong.

**Swap test.** Replace the brand or author name with a competitor's. If the line still works, it doesn't differentiate. Essential for positioning, taglines, manifestos, and thought leadership.

**So-what chain.** Feature → "so what?" → advantage → "so what?" → the benefit the reader cares about. Stop where the reader would stop asking. Diagnosis for feature lists; also the reverse check: a benefit with no feature behind it is an unsupported claim.

**Obvious-thesis test.** Would any reasonable person argue the opposite? If not, it isn't a point of view. Essential for thought leadership and manifestos.

## Structure

**Inverted pyramid / BLUF.** Most important information first, details after. For news, corporate communications, memos, and emails.

**SCQA** (after Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*). Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. For investor writing, proposals, and strategy documents; diagnose whether the complication is stated before the answer arrives.

**Persuasion sequences as diagnostics.** AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action), PAS (problem, agitation, solution), and BAB (before, after, bridge) are templates for drafting. Use them to diagnose, not to impose: does the reader recognize the problem before the solution appears? Is there a reason to act before the ask? Don't force a manifesto or a white paper into them.

**Narrative arc.** Tension, turn, resolution. For manifestos, thought leadership, scripts, and long-form editorial: is there a tension worth resolving, and does the ending resolve it?

**Reader's questions.** List the questions this reader brings, in the order they'd ask them. Compare with the piece's order. Most hierarchy problems show up as an answer arriving before its question, or a question never answered.

## Language

**Plain language** (Google developer documentation style guide, CC BY 4.0; Microsoft Writing Style Guide; plainlanguage.gov). Write for the reader's task. Use second person and present tense for instructions, active voice when the actor matters, one idea per sentence where clarity is at stake, the reader's own words over internal terms, and consistent terminology. Plain doesn't mean simplistic: precise technical terms are plain to the reader who uses them.

**Read-aloud test.** Read the piece aloud at speaking pace. Stumbles mark clarity problems; a voice that sounds like nobody marks a voice problem. Essential for scripts and taglines.

## Persuasion ethics

**Influence principles** (after Robert Cialdini, *Influence*): reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity. Use them to name what a persuasive move relies on. The line between persuasion and manipulation: the principle is applied with true information the reader would endorse knowing. Fake scarcity, invented social proof, and borrowed authority cross it; see [claims.md](claims.md).

# Claim integrity

Credibility problems are the most expensive problems copy can have: a misleading claim damages trust, can create legal exposure, and survives every sentence-level polish. Check claims before style.

## 1. Inventory the claims

List every statement that asserts something checkable or promises something. Classify each:

| Type | Example | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **Factual** | "Founded in 2011." "Ships in 48 hours." | To be true. |
| **Quantified** | "3× faster." "Saves 6 hours a week." | A baseline (faster than what?), a source, and a method. |
| **Comparative / superlative** | "The fastest." "The only." "#1." "Better than X." | Substantiation the author holds. Regulated in most advertising codes. |
| **Outcome / promise** | "You'll close more deals." "Sleep better tonight." | A plausible mechanism, and honesty about who gets that result. |
| **Social proof** | "Trusted by 10,000 teams." Testimonials. Logos. | To be real, current, and representative. |
| **Belief / opinion** | "We think software should be boring." | To be framed as belief. Fine without proof. |

## 2. Place each claim on the evidence ladder

1. **Supported in the material:** the evidence is in the piece or in documents the user supplied.
2. **Asserted by the author:** the user states it is true; the piece doesn't show it. Usually fine; note where showing it would persuade more.
3. **Plausible but unsupported:** neither shown nor stated. Flag it; suggest the proof that would carry it, as a `[NEEDS: …]` placeholder.
4. **Unsupportable or misleading:** cannot be true as written, or is true in a way that misleads. P0.

Never move a claim up the ladder yourself. You cannot verify a claim by finding it plausible.

## 3. Misleading patterns (P0 when present)

- **Unanchored comparisons:** "3× faster," "50% less," "up to 10×" with no baseline, or with an "up to" that describes a rare best case.
- **False precision:** "97.3% of users" with no study behind it.
- **Implied guarantees:** outcome language with no qualification where results vary ("Double your revenue").
- **Typical-results deception:** a best-case testimonial presented as what the reader should expect.
- **Unsupported superlatives:** "the only," "the first," "the leading," "#1" without a stated basis.
- **Borrowed authority:** "experts agree," "studies show," "research proves" with no named source.
- **Manufactured urgency or scarcity:** countdowns, "only 3 left," "price goes up tonight" that aren't true.
- **Euphemism that hides material facts:** layoffs, price increases, data use, or risks described so the affected reader won't understand.
- **Selective framing:** a time window, cohort, or metric chosen because it flatters, presented as representative.
- **Fine print that reverses the headline.**

A claim can be persuasive and misleading at once. Persuasive power is not a defence; report the integrity problem first and explain the trust and legal cost.

## 4. Regulated territory

Health, medical, and wellness claims; financial returns and investor communications; environmental and sustainability claims ("carbon neutral," "eco-friendly," "sustainable"); comparative advertising; claims aimed at children. Flag these for substantiation and legal review. Do not give legal advice and do not certify a claim as compliant.

## 5. How to fix

- Make the claim smaller and true: "Most teams finish setup in under an hour" instead of "Setup in minutes."
- Anchor numbers: "3× faster than our previous version at importing 1 GB CSV files" instead of "3× faster."
- Swap adjectives for proof the author already has: a named customer, a specific result, a demonstration.
- Reframe opinion as opinion: "We think" is honest and often more persuasive than a fake fact.
- When the honest version needs data the user hasn't supplied, write both: the honest version now, and the stronger version with a `[NEEDS: …]` placeholder.
